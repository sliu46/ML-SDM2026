\begin{abstract}
%% Text of abstract

Species distribution models (SDM) have become important tools for biodiversity assessment, ecological prediction, and conservation planning. Recent advances in machine learning (ML) have greatly expanded the capability of SDM to analyze complex ecological data. However, the overall development trajectory, knowledge structure, and methodological implications of machine learning-based SDM (ML-SDM) remain insufficiently synthesized. This study provides a comprehensive review of ML-SDM research from 2006 to 2025 by integrating bibliometric analysis with methodological synthesis. Using 5,224 articles retrieved from the Web of Science Core Collection, we combined general bibliometric statistics, collaboration analysis, co-citation analysis, bibliographic coupling, temporal keyword evolution, and qualitative synthesis to investigate research trends, methodological development, knowledge accumulation, and future challenges. The results reveal that ML-SDM has undergone a methodological evolution rather than a simple transition from traditional ML to deep learning (DL). Early studies established the methodological foundation through MaxEnt, Random Forest, and other ML algorithms, followed by advances in model evaluation, uncertainty assessment, and ecological applicability. Recent developments increasingly emphasize DL, ensemble modelling, remote sensing, and multi-source environmental data, reflecting an expansion from improving predictive accuracy toward modelling increasingly complex ecological information. The observed development and global dissemination patterns of ML-SDM are associated with broader ecological demands, technological advances, expanding environmental data resources, and increasing international collaboration, which may have provided enabling conditions for methodological innovation and knowledge exchange. Co-citation and bibliographic coupling analyses further suggest that methodological development has progressed through successive efforts to address key modelling challenges. Although DL-SDM expands the capacity of ML-SDM to analyse high-dimensional and spatially structured ecological data, it remains primarily a correlative modelling framework and does not replace traditional ML approaches. Challenges related to ecological interpretation, uncertainty assessment, spatial transferability, and model transparency continue to limit its practical application.



\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{introduction}

With technological development, intensified human activities, and land-use change, species extinction rates have exceeded natural background levels \citep{ceballos2017biological}. Therefore, understanding biodiversity patterns and supporting effective conservation decision-making have become major global research priorities \citep{maxwell2016biodiversity}. Since the 1990s, the international community has gradually established a global biodiversity governance system centered on the Convention on Biological Diversity. This convention was adopted in 1992 and came into effect in 1993 \citep{CBD1993}. Subsequently, the 2010 Biodiversity Target and the Aichi Biodiversity Targets further emphasized measurable objectives, indicators, and time-bound conservation actions \citep{CBD2010}. However, the 2010 Biodiversity Target was not achieved, with global biodiversity indicators continuing to decline, highlighting the limitations of existing biodiversity monitoring and assessment frameworks \citep{butchart2010global}. Then, the United Nations adopted the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework in 2022, which established new global biodiversity targets and emphasized implementation, monitoring, and effectiveness evaluation in conservation actions \citep{CBD2022}.

Along with increasing biodiversity conservation demands and global environmental governance initiatives, species distribution model (SDM) have become increasingly important tools for biodiversity assessment, habitat evaluation, and conservation decision-making \citep{elith2009species}. SDM is a modeling approach used to estimate the potential distribution of target species based on species occurrence data, environmental variables, and their relationships \citep{xu2015development,franklin2013species}. The methodological evolution of SDM has been primarily driven by advances in ecological theory, statistical modeling, geographic information science (GIS), computational techniques, and the increasing availability of environmental data, while biodiversity policies have mainly promoted their application in conservation assessment and decision-making \citep{austin2007species, guisan2005predicting}. Therefore, reviewing the evolution process of its technology is of great significance for understanding the development logic of SDM.

The maturation of GIS and advances in spatial analysis during the 1990s provided important technical foundations for spatial ecological research and environmental modelling \citep{goodchild1992geographical}. Meanwhile, statistical modelling approaches, particularly generalized linear model (GLM), became widely adopted for relating species distributions to environmental variables and contributed to the development of early SDM frameworks \citep{guisan2000predictive}. Subsequently, advances in remote sensing and big data technologies have promoted the application of large-scale environmental datasets in ecological research since the early 21st century \citep{chen2022remote}. Since the 2010s, advances in high-resolution remote sensing data, graphics processing unit (GPU)-based parallel computing, and deep learning (DL) have substantially enhanced the computational capacity for complex spatial modelling \citep{owens2008gpu,zhu2017deep}. Since 2020, the development of geospatial artificial intelligence and advanced DL approaches has further accelerated the evolution of data-driven spatial analysis methods\citep{janowicz2020geoai,reichstein2019deep}.

Therefore, SDM can be broadly classified into three major methodological categories: (1) Statistical model–based SDM mainly rely on methods such as GLM and generalized additive model to characterize the relationships between species distributions and environmental variables. (2) SDM based on traditional ML (TML-SDM) represented by random forest and MaxEnt emphasize nonlinear modeling capabilities and improved predictive performance. (3) SDM based on DL (DL-SDM), such as convolutional neural networks (CNN) and deep neural networks (DNN), enable automatic feature extraction and high-dimensional data processing, improving the ability to capture complex nonlinear relationships and spatial patterns from large and heterogeneous ecological datasets \citep{guisan2000predictive,elith2006novel,robinson2018correcting}. Given that the development and application of statistical model-based SDM have been extensively reviewed in previous studies, this study focuses specifically on machine learning (ML)-based SDM (ML-SDM), including traditional ML and DL approaches, to investigate their technological evolution and research trends \citep{elith2009species, guisan2000predictive} (Table \ref{tab:sdm_models}). Among these categories, DL-SDM refers to a specific class of ML-SDM that applies DL methodologies to species distribution modelling. Following the definition of DL, DL-SDM employ modular ML architectures that learn both decision functions and features for species distribution prediction in a layer-wise fashion. The key distinction of DL-SDM from TML-SDM lies in the use of multi-layer learning architectures that enable simultaneous learning of features and decision functions through a layer-wise process \citep{kellenberger2026performance, lecun2015deep}.

For SDM that heavily relies on multi-source data, algorithmic methods, and computing technologies, a single disciplinary perspective is no longer sufficient to comprehensively grasp the technical development trajectory \citep{guisan2005predicting}. Therefore, a scientific bibliometric analysis based on literature data provides a data-driven research approach for systematically identifying research hotspots, technological evolution paths, and changes in knowledge structures \citep{li2022scientometric,chen2017science}. Moreover, previous studies have attempted to systematically synthesize the development of SDM. For instance, \cite{pecchi2019species} starting from the practical needs of forest management, explored datasets of species occurrence, climate and soil prediction variables, modeling algorithms, evaluation methods, and widely used software in SDM. \cite{vasconcelos2024advances} adopted a mixed review to summarize the most commonly used modeling methods in SDM, the trends of international cooperation, and technological trends. \cite{kroth2025habitat} focused on large aquatic plants in lakes and systematically evaluated the application characteristics of SDM in aquatic ecosystems. \cite{tumer2026two} reviewed SDM applied in forest ecosystems against the backdrop of climate change.

Most of these existing reviews focus on specific ecosystems, application scenarios, or research objectives, providing valuable summaries of SDM methodologies and applications. However, systematic quantitative analyses of the overall evolution process of the SDM remain limited. Unlike previous review articles, this study systematically analyzes the technological evolution of ML-SDM by integrating developments in related disciplines with the broader context of biodiversity conservation. By focusing on SDM studies involving ML and DL, we identify the emergence of ML-SDM research in 2006. Therefore, the study period is set from 2006 to 2025, focusing on the evolution process of the ML-SDM research. Meanwhile, we conduct a comprehensive analysis of the research landscape of ML-SDM at four levels: national, author, institution, and journal. \section{Materials and methods}

This paper employs VOSviewer (version 1.6.20), HistCite Pro (version 2.1), RStudio (version 2026.01.0), and the Bibliometrix R package (version 5.2.1) with its Biblioshiny interface for bibliometric analysis and visualization. VOSviewer is used to construct and visualize keyword co-occurrence networks, co-citation networks, and bibliographic coupling networks. It provides effective visualization and interpretation of large-scale bibliometric networks structures and visualizing temporal information \citep{van2010software,van2014visualizing}. HistCite Pro is mainly used to calculate citation metrics such as Total Local Citation Score (TLCS) and Total Global Citation Score (TGCS), facilitating the identification of highly cited publications and the characterization of citation patterns across research entities \citep{garfield2002algorithmic}. RStudio serves as the development environment for executing customized R scripts, which are used for the generation of customized bibliometric figures, including publication trends, collaboration networks, author productivity, and keyword evolution. The Bibliometrix R package and its Biblioshiny interface are used for bibliometric statistical analysis and for generating three-field plots and other descriptive bibliometric visualizations \citep{aria2017bibliometrix}. \subsection{Data collection}

Web of Science Core Collection (WoS) is widely employed in bibliometric research and provides standardized bibliographic and citation information \citep{chen2017science,mongeon2016journal}. Previous cross-disciplinary comparisons have shown that the proportion of highly cited documents missing from Web of Science is substantially lower in the natural, life, and health sciences than in the social sciences and humanities \citep{martin2018coverage}. Therefore, WoS was selected as the bibliographic data source in this study to ensure consistency in bibliographic metadata and citation relationships.

The search strategy for this article is: TS = ("Species Distribution Model*" OR "Ecological Niche Model*" OR "Habitat Suitability Model*") AND ("machine learning" OR "MaxEnt" OR "Random Forest" OR "deep learning" OR "Convolutional Neural Network" OR "Graph Neural Network" OR "Multilayer Perceptron" OR "Transformer"). Based on the above search strategy, 5,788 documents were retrieved and used to form the initial dataset. Review papers were excluded because they mainly synthesize previously published studies and may differ from original research articles in citation characteristics. Conference papers were also excluded because their indexing and publication standards may vary across research fields. Therefore, only articles were retained for the bibliometric analysis. The time span covered 2006 to 2025. The year 2006 was selected as the starting year because sustained publication activity related to ML-SDM became evident from this period under the adopted search strategy. After applying the document type and publication year filters in WoS, 564 records were excluded, leaving 5,224 publications for the final analysis. No additional manual screening or duplicate-removal procedure was conducted.


\subsection{Data analysis}

After literature screening, a bibliometric framework was constructed to analyze the development and evolution of SDM. 

(1) Research landscape and potential enabling conditions.

A bibliometric analysis was conducted to characterize the overall research landscape and identify potential ecological, technological, and collaborative conditions associated with the development of ML-SDM. Annual publication output, citation trends, and the contributions of countries, institutions, authors, and journals were analyzed to characterize research productivity, citation patterns, and participation in ML-SDM research. Collaboration relationships among countries, authors, institutions, and journals were further examined to reveal global knowledge production and dissemination networks. These quantitative results were subsequently integrated with keyword evolution and qualitative synthesis to interpret ecological, technological, and collaborative conditions potentially associated with the emergence and global dissemination of ML-SDM.

(2) Methodological evolution and knowledge accumulation

A combination of co-citation analysis and bibliographic coupling analysis was employed to investigate the evolution of the methodological knowledge base of ML-SDM. Co-citation analysis was used to identify influential references and reveal the intellectual foundations of the field, whereas bibliographic coupling analysis characterized recent methodological developments and emerging research structures. Representative methodological studies identified through these analyses were further synthesized to reconstruct the evolutionary trajectory of ML-SDM.

(3) Evolution toward DL-SDM: opportunities and limitations

The transition toward DL-SDM was investigated through an integrated analysis of keyword evolution, representative methodological studies, and qualitative synthesis. Temporal keyword co-occurrence analysis was used to identify emerging research themes related to DL, remote sensing, ensemble modelling, and advanced computational approaches. Representative studies were further examined to evaluate how DL expanded the modelling capability of traditional ML-SDM. Finally, qualitative synthesis was performed to assess the opportunities and limitations of DL-SDM, with particular emphasis on ecological interpretation, uncertainty assessment, data requirements, and spatial transferability.



\subsection{Methodology}

Numerical indexes are first introduced to help readers understand key concepts in the subsequent analysis. Then, three network-based research methods are employed: co-citation, bibliographic coupling, and keyword co-occurrence analysis.

\subsubsection{Numerical indexes}

For the majority of scholars, the impact factor (IF) is a well-known metric for measuring the citation impact of academic journals. It is a journal-level metric calculated from the average number of citations received by articles published in a journal during a specific period \citep{garfield2006history}. However, IF may be influenced by disciplinary differences, journal scope, and citation practices and therefore should not be used as a direct measure of the scientific quality of individual papers or researchers. Citation-based indicators were used in this study to describe citation patterns within the analyzed literature and in the broader scientific literature \citep{mongeon2016journal,moed2005citation}. The TLCS represents the number of times a publication is cited by other publications within the literature collection analyzed in this study, whereas the TGCS represents the total number of citations received by a publication in the broader WoS database \citep{garfield2009science, fan2023data}. Accordingly, TLCS reflects citation frequency within the analyzed ML-SDM literature, whereas TGCS reflects broader citation visibility in the WoS database. Because citation counts accumulate over time, raw TLCS and TGCS values are strongly influenced by publication age. Earlier publications generally have longer citation windows and therefore more opportunities to accumulate citations than recently published studies. Consequently, direct comparisons of raw citation totals across publication years should be interpreted with caution. In this study, TLCS and TGCS are therefore treated as descriptive indicators of citation activity and scholarly visibility rather than direct measures of research quality or methodological importance. Citation frequency may provide indirect evidence of the extent to which a publication has been recognized, discussed, or used by subsequent research, but citation-based indicators alone cannot capture all dimensions of research quality or methodological contribution \citep{aksnes2019citations,hicks2015bibliometrie}. Accordingly, these indicators were used primarily to describe citation activity and scholarly visibility, while the methodological significance of representative studies was evaluated separately through qualitative examination of their substantive contributions.

\subsubsection{Network-based methods}

To reveal the knowledge base and thematic evolution of SDM research, we applied a bibliometric framework based on three bibliometric network methods: co-citation, bibliographic coupling, and keyword co-occurrence analysis. \citep{chen2017science,van2010software,van2014visualizing}.

The nodes and links in the co-citation network are the cited documents and the co-citation relationships. This type of network is based on the investigation of bibliographic data sets through citation relationships. When a paper cites two references, a link is established between them, with link strength indicating their co-citation frequency. Co-citation analysis helps identify influential literature and theoretical foundations in a field. Therefore, it is suitable for revealing the knowledge base and theoretical framework of SDM research. \citep{garfield2002algorithmic,garfield2009science}.

Bibliographic coupling analysis takes the paper itself as the node. When two papers share common references, this indicates similarities in their methodological or intellectual foundations. As bibliographic coupling relationships are formed at publication, this method is particularly suitable for revealing current research structures and recent trends \citep{chen2017science,kessler1963bibliographic}.

Keyword co-occurrence analysis is a thematic analysis method that focuses on the keywords of a paper as the research object. When two keywords appear in the same paper, it is considered that there is a co-occurrence relationship between them. The keyword co-occurrence network reflects the relationships among research topics and the distribution of research hotspots. It is an effective method for revealing the thematic structure and evolution of a research field. The research focus and its trend over time can be identified by analyzing the co-occurrence relationships of keywords \citep{aria2017bibliometrix}. Before network construction, synonymous and variant keyword forms were standardized using a VOSviewer thesaurus file. The complete keyword-mapping file is available in the associated GitHub repository to support reproducibility.

\subsubsection{Selection and methodological coding of representative studies and modelling approaches}

Representative methodological studies were identified using a year-stratified, two-stage procedure that combined bibliometric candidate identification with independent content-based assessment \citep{zupic2015bibliometric, donthu2021conduct, linnenluecke2020conducting}. The procedure was designed to reduce the influence of differences in citation accumulation among publication years and to provide continuous coverage of methodological development during 2006-2025.

(1) Candidate studies were identified separately for each publication year. Publications were ranked according to TGCS and TLCS. Studies appearing within the top five for both indicators in the same year were retained as candidates for methodological assessment. TGCS and TLCS were used only for candidate identification within each annual stratum. They were not treated as direct measures of research quality, methodological importance, or scientific contribution. The complete candidate set, together with publication year, TGCS, and TLCS, is provided in the associated data repository.

(2) All candidate studies were independently assessed by two reviewers using predefined methodological eligibility criteria. A study was considered eligible when it made an explicit contribution to at least one aspect of ML-SDM methodology. Eligible contributions included: introduction or adaptation of a modelling framework; model calibration, complexity control, or parameter optimization; treatment of sampling bias, background data, or pseudo-absence selection; model evaluation, threshold selection, spatial validation, or transferability assessment; uncertainty assessment; representation or integration of spatial, remote-sensing, or other complex environmental data; and joint or multispecies modelling. Studies that only applied an established modelling approach to a specific species, taxonomic group, or geographic region were excluded when they did not provide substantive methodological development or evaluation. One representative methodological study was retained from each annual candidate set to ensure continuous temporal coverage across 2006--2025. The final selection was based on the clarity and relevance of the methodological contribution rather than citation rank alone. This annual selection was intended to represent methodological development over time rather than to identify the single most important study in each year.

For each retained study, information was extracted using a standardized coding framework. Three items were recorded: modelling approach and data representation, methodological issue addressed, and principal methodological contribution. The two reviewers conducted the assessment and coding independently. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Meanwhile, representative modelling approaches were synthesized from the selected methodological studies and additional methodological or comparative literature. Approaches were included when they represented distinct learning mechanisms or data-representation strategies and when their model-specific capabilities and constraints were sufficiently characterized in the literature. Closely related variants were grouped when they shared similar learning principles, whereas application-specific implementations were not considered independent methodological categories. Limitations common to most correlative SDMs were excluded from model-specific comparisons.

Annual publication output provides an indicator of research activity, whereas citation-based metrics describe patterns of citation accumulation and scholarly visibility \citep{donthu2021conduct}. In this subsection, we analyzed annual publication trends and temporal variations in TLCS and TGCS to characterize publication dynamics and citation accumulation patterns in ML-SDM research (Figure \ref{fig_mom1}). The annual number of publications represents the number of SDM-related records indexed in the WoS database each year. TLCS and TGCS were used to describe citation frequency within the analyzed ML-SDM literature and broader citation visibility in the Web of Science database, respectively.

The results showed that the annual publication output in the ML-SDM research field increased continuously from 2006 to 2025. The number of publications increased from 5 papers in 2006 to 821 papers in 2025, indicating a rapid expansion of research activity. During 2006–2010, the publication output remained relatively low, increasing gradually from 5 to 63 papers. After 2011, the annual number of publications continued to increase and exceeded 100 papers for the first time in 2011. The publication output further increased, reaching 222 papers in 2016 and 294 papers in 2019. After 2020, the research output entered a period of rapid growth, with annual publications increasing from 387 papers in 2020 to 821 papers in 2025. The highest publication output during the study period was recorded in 2025 (Figure \ref{fig_mom1},Appendix \ref{tab:3.1pro}).

Although publication counts describe research output, citation counts provide a different perspective on the extent to which publications have been cited within and beyond the analyzed literature. TGCS values were generally higher than TLCS values because TGCS includes citations recorded in the broader WoS database, whereas TLCS only includes citations originating from publications within the analyzed ML-SDM dataset. Because both indicators accumulate over time, differences among publication years should be interpreted in light of their unequal citation windows.

Compared with the continuous increase in publication output, TGCS showed considerable fluctuations during the study period. The highest TGCS value was observed in 2006 (16,902 citations), followed by a decline to 3,951 citations in 2008. From 2009 to 2011, TGCS increased to 13,138 citations in 2011 and reached another high value of 14,594 citations in 2014. After 2015, TGCS increased from 6,511 citations in 2015 to more than 11,000 citations during 2020–2021, before declining to 1,674 citations in 2025. TLCS also varied substantially across years. The highest TLCS value was observed in 2006 (4,233 citations), followed by lower values during 2007–2010. TLCS increased again in 2011 to 3,295 citations and reached 2,483 citations in 2014. After 2015, accumulated TLCS values were generally lower, decreasing from 1,453 citations in 2016 to 31 citations in 2025. Earlier publication years generally accumulated higher TLCS values, whereas recent publication years showed lower accumulated citation totals, partly reflecting their shorter citation windows (Figure \ref{fig_mom1}).

By comparing the temporal variations of TGCS and TLCS, several publication years with relatively high accumulated citation totals were identified. The most prominent values occurred in 2006, when TGCS and TLCS reached 16,902 and 4,233 citations, respectively. Relatively high values were also observed in 2011 and 2014, with TGCS/TLCS values of 13,138/3,295 and 14,594/2,483, respectively. In addition, TGCS increased notably around 2021, whereas the corresponding accumulated TLCS remained lower than those of earlier peak years. Given the shorter citation window of publications from this period, this difference should not be interpreted as evidence of lower importance or weaker recognition within the ML-SDM field.

A further review of highly cited publications associated with these peak years showed that a small number of studies contributed substantially to the total citations accumulated by papers published in those years. In 2006, “Maximum entropy modeling of species geographic distributions” \citep{phillips2006maximum} received 14,211 TGCS and 3,443 TLCS, accounting for a large proportion of the citation totals accumulated by publications from that year. In 2011, “A statistical explanation of MaxEnt for ecologists” \citep{elith2011statistical} received 5,479 TGCS and 1,759 TLCS and similarly contributed substantially to the relatively high citation totals observed for that publication year. In 2014, “ENMeval: An R package for conducting spatially independent evaluations and estimating optimal model complexity for MAXENT ecological niche models” \citep{muscarella2014enm} received 1,832 TGCS and 618 TLCS, while “Spatial filtering to reduce sampling bias can improve the performance of ecological niche models” \citep{boria2014spatial} received 1,190 TGCS and 354 TLCS. These highly cited studies were associated with major methodological developments in ML-SDM, including MaxEnt modelling, statistical interpretation, model evaluation, parameter optimization, and sampling-bias correction. A similar pattern was observed around 2021. “ENMeval 2.0: Redesigned for customizable and reproducible modelling of species’ niches and distributions” \citep{kass2021enmeval} received 639 TGCS and 188 TLCS, while “ENMTools 1.0: an R package for comparative ecological biogeography” \citep{warren2021enmtools} received 366 TGCS and 76 TLCS. Together, these studies contributed to the relatively high accumulated citation totals observed for publications from this period. Overall, the citation peaks were therefore strongly influenced by a limited number of highly cited studies, several of which addressed methodological developments in ML-SDM.

Figure \ref{fig_mom9} illustrates the co-citation network of highly cited references in ML-SDM research, which is organized into four major clusters. Although the clusters are interconnected rather than completely separated, each is centered on a group of frequently co-cited studies that together represent different components of the methodological knowledge base underlying ML-SDM.

The red cluster is mainly associated with methodological issues involved in model specification and practical implementation, including model evaluation, sampling and background selection, model complexity, parameter settings, and environmental predictors. \cite{elith2009species} reviewed the ecological and methodological basis of SDMs and discussed issues related to model selection, evaluation, and prediction across space and time, while \cite{allouche2006assessing} introduced the True Skill Statistic as an alternative measure for evaluating presence absence predictions. \cite{phillips2009sample} examined the effects of sample-selection bias and background sampling in presence-only modelling, whereas \cite{warren2011ecological} and \cite{merow2013practical} addressed model complexity, regularization, input data, and parameter settings in MaxEnt. \cite{fick2017worldclim} provided the WorldClim 2 high-resolution global climate surfaces that can serve as environmental predictors in species distribution modelling. The cluster represents methodological considerations associated with model formulation, calibration, evaluation, and environmental data preparation.

The green cluster contains frequently co-cited references related to comparative modelling frameworks, ensemble prediction, and evaluation of alternative modelling approaches.These references reflect methodological comparisons among different modelling strategies rather than a specific algorithmic trajectory.  Representative studies include "A review of methods for the assessment of prediction errors in conservation presence/absence models" \citep{fielding1997review}, "Random forests" \citep{breiman2001random}, "Novel methods improve prediction of species' distributions from occurrence data" \citep{elith2006novel}, and "Ensemble forecasting of species distributions" \citep{araujo2007ensemble}. \cite{fielding1997review} reviewed methods for assessing prediction errors in presence-absence models, including measures based on classification performance and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. \cite{breiman2001random} introduced Random Forest as an ensemble of randomized decision trees with internal procedures for error estimation and variable-importance assessment. \cite{elith2006novel} compared a broad range of modelling methods for predicting species distributions from occurrence data and evaluated their predictions using independent presence--absence data. \cite{araujo2007ensemble} examined variation among alternative species-distribution forecasts and discussed the use of multiple models within an ensemble forecasting framework. Taken together, these studies reflect the use and comparison of different predictive approaches, together with methods for evaluating variation in model performance and predictions.

The blue cluster is centred on core references related to MaxEnt species distribution prediction, together with studies addressing statistical interpretation, limited occurrence data, and prediction assessment. \cite{phillips2006maximum} introduced MaxEnt as an approach for predicting species geographic distributions from presence-only records, and \cite{elith2011statistical} later clarified its statistical basis and the roles of features, regularization, and background data. \cite{pearson2007predicting} examined species-distribution prediction when only small numbers of occurrence records were available, providing an empirical assessment of modelling under limited-data conditions. \cite{swets1988measuring} originating from the broader field of diagnostic assessment, described receiver operating characteristic analysis for evaluating discriminatory accuracy, an approach subsequently used widely in ecological model evaluation. Their co-citation indicates that MaxEnt-related modelling studies were closely connected with statistical interpretation, occurrence-data limitations, and predictive evaluation within the ML-SDM knowledge base.

The yellow cluster contains references that primarily support environmental niche modelling through climatic data resources and analytical procedures for model evaluation and niche comparison. \cite{hijmans2005very} developed high-resolution interpolated climate surfaces for global land areas, providing spatially explicit climatic variables for ecological and biogeographic analyses. Model evaluation was examined by \cite{peterson2008rethinking}, who discussed limitations of conventional ROC and area-under-the-curve (AUC) applications in ecological niche modelling and proposed a partial ROC approach for evaluating model predictions. Other references in this cluster concern quantitative comparisons among environmental niches. \cite{warren2008environmental} developed methods for measuring niche overlap and testing niche equivalency and similarity under different null hypotheses, while \cite{warren2010enmtools} introduced ENMTools to facilitate quantitative comparisons of environmental niche models and associated randomization tests. These references thus relate less to the introduction of a particular predictive algorithm and more to the data and analytical procedures used to evaluate and compare environmental niche models.

Bibliographic coupling analysis was used to examine the shared reference structure among ML-SDM source publications and to characterize more recent research connections \citep{zupic2015bibliometric, donthu2021conduct}. No minimum citation threshold was imposed, because raw citation-based filtering may disproportionately exclude recently published studies with shorter citation accumulation periods. From the full corpus of 5,224 publications, the 300 documents with the highest total link strength were retained to represent the strongly connected backbone of the overall ML-SDM literature. Total link strength measures the summed strength of an item’s links with all other items in the network and is commonly used in VOSviewer to characterize network connectivity \citep{boyack2010co}. To ensure that recent DL-SDM studies were not lost during this network-based reduction, all DL-related articles identified using the predefined search: ("Species Distribution Model*" OR "Ecological Niche Model*" OR "Habitat Suitability Model*") AND ("deep learning" OR "Convolutional Neural Network" OR "Graph Neural Network" OR "Multilayer Perceptron" OR "Transformer") were additionally retained. This search identified 61 DL-related articles. The final focused dataset therefore comprised 361 publications, consisting of the 300 documents with the highest total link strength from the overall corpus and all 61 predefined DL-related articles \citep{jarneving2007bibliographic,klavans2011global}. (Figure \ref{fig_mom10}).

Among the earlier representative studies highlighted in the network, \cite{kramer2013importance} and \cite{muscarella2014enm} focused on methodological issues in MaxEnt-based species distribution modelling. \cite{kramer2013importance}, in The importance of correcting for sampling bias in MaxEnt species distribution models, compared spatial filtering and background manipulation for reducing sampling bias in presence-only data. The study showed that accounting for uneven sampling effort can reduce omission and commission errors. \cite{muscarella2014enm} introduced the framework, which supports spatially independent data partitioning, tuning of MaxEnt settings, and model evaluation using multiple criteria. Both studies are well connected within the focused bibliographic coupling network, indicating substantial overlap in cited methodological literature with other ML-SDM publications.

Representative studies published between 2018 and 2020 focused on predictor selection, collinearity, model evaluation, and comparisons among modelling approaches. \cite{de2018evaluating} used virtual species simulations to examine the effects of predictor collinearity on SDM performance. The results showed that collinearity can reduce model efficiency and increase uncertainty, although its effects differed among modelling algorithms. \cite{fourcade2018paintings} examined environmental predictor selection and commonly used evaluation statistics. The study showed that high AUC or true skill statistic values do not necessarily indicate biologically realistic predictions. \cite{feng2019collinearity} further distinguished predictor collinearity from collinearity shifts and environmental novelty. The latter two factors reduced MaxEnt performance during model transfer. \cite{kaky2020comparison} compared ensemble modelling with MaxEnt and found comparable predictive performance in the examined conservation case. These studies are well connected within the focused network, indicating substantial overlap in the references used to address model reliability and evaluation.

More recent representative studies addressed model reliability, benchmarking, and deep-learning-based SDMs. \cite{santini2021assessing} evaluated the reliability of species distribution projections under climate-change scenarios. The study examined the effects of modelling choices and violations of modelling assumptions using virtual species. \cite{valavi2022predictive} compared 21 approaches for presence-only SDMs and provided reproducible code for model benchmarking. In parallel, \cite{deneu2021convolutional} applied convolutional neural networks to capture the spatial structure of environmental data in species distribution modelling. \cite{zbinden2024selection} examined the selection of pseudo-absences in deep-learning-based SDMs and evaluated the effects of class imbalance and geographic bias. These recent studies remain connected to the broader ML-SDM literature through shared references. Their positions in the network indicate overlap in cited knowledge bases rather than a separate methodological.

The keyword co-occurrence network can reveal the main research topics and their evolutionary characteristics within a research field. Author keywords were selected for analysis because they directly reflect the core research topics addressed in each paper. Therefore, keywords represent nodes and their co-occurrences form the links in this network. 

Figure \ref{fig_mom11} identified the major research themes and their temporal evolution in ML-SDM research. The network was constructed from 500 high-frequency author keywords, revealing a dense knowledge structure centered on sdm, MaxEnt, and climate change. These three keywords formed the largest nodes and showed the strongest connections with other topics, indicating that species distribution modelling, the MaxEnt algorithm, and climate change have remained the fundamental research framework throughout the development of ML-SDM. Several methodological keywords, including ML, random forest, remote sensing, ensemble modeling, and DL, were closely connected with the central SDM theme. Environmental and ecological topics such as bioclimatic variables, biodiversity, conservation planning, ecological niche, biological invasions, and threatened species also occupied important positions in the network. These results indicate that ML-SDM research is a integrated studies that combine algorithm development with biodiversity conservation, climate change assessment, and ecological management. Since approximately 2016, ML algorithms, remote sensing, and ensemble modeling have become increasingly important. DL appeared as one of the most recent keywords and remained located at the periphery of the overall network, suggesting that DL-SDM is an emerging research direction. Although its occurrence frequency is still considerably lower than that of traditional approaches, its direct connections with ML, remote sensing, random forest, and habitat suitability indicate integration with existing ML-SDM methodologies.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Research landscape and potential enabling conditions}

The rapid expansion of ML-SDM during the past two decades coincided with broader changes in ecological demands, data availability, and computational technology \citep{elith2009species,guisan2005predicting}. Growing concerns about climate change, biodiversity loss, biological invasions, and ecosystem conservation have highlighted the need for accurate and scalable species distribution prediction \citep{pacifici2017species,hooper2012global}. Traditional statistical models may have limited flexibility in representing complex nonlinear relationships and high-dimensional environmental variables. Therefore, ML approaches have been increasingly explored in SDM research due to their ability to represent complex relationships and improve predictive performance \citep{pichler2023machine}. The availability of ecological data also changed substantially during this period. Open biodiversity databases such as global biodiversity information facility and global environmental datasets such as WorldClim provided large volumes of standardized occurrence records and climate variables \citep{telenius2011biodiversity,fick2017worldclim}. At the same time, advances in remote sensing products provided richer environmental information across multiple spatial scales \citep{wulder2004high}. These developments expanded the availability of environmental predictors and coincided with the application of ML-SDM from regional case studies toward broader biodiversity assessments \citep{reichstein2019deep}. Advances in computing infrastructure also provided additional support for the application of increasingly complex ML models. GPU computing, together with open-source frameworks such as TensorFlow and PyTorch, reduced the technical barriers associated with developing and applying complex ML models \citep{owens2008gpu,abadi2016tensorflow,paszke2019pytorch}. Widely used SDM tools, including MaxEnt, ENMeval, and biomod2, also improved model accessibility, reproducibility, and methodological standardization\citep{phillips2006maximum, kass2021enmeval, thuiller2009biomod}. These resources reduced technical barriers for ecological researchers and may have facilitated the broader application of ML approaches. Therefore, the observed growth of ML-SDM should not be interpreted simply as increasing research activity. Instead, it reflects the concurrent development of ecological data infrastructure, computational resources, and methodological innovation. Together, these factors may have provided enabling conditions for the subsequent exploration of DL and more advanced ecological prediction frameworks.

The international collaboration network reflects more than publication activity. It provides a bibliometric representation of international research connections associated with the dissemination of ML-SDM research. The United States, China, and several European countries occupy central positions in the collaboration network, indicating that methodological development is supported by extensive international cooperation rather than by isolated national efforts. Researchers from different regions jointly develop algorithms, validate models under diverse ecological conditions, and expand ML approaches from regional case studies to global biodiversity applications. International collaboration is particularly important for ML-SDM because species distribution modelling depends on environmental gradients, biodiversity databases, and climate datasets that extend beyond national boundaries. Shared occurrence records, standardized environmental variables, and open modelling frameworks allow new methods to be evaluated across different ecosystems and geographical regions. The collaboration network therefore represents scientific partnerships and the global diffusion of ML-SDM methodologies. These networks may provide opportunities for methodological comparison and evaluation across regions, but actual validation requires empirical assessments using independent datasets, spatial cross-validation, and uncertainty analysis. 

Growing ecological challenges have also promoted the international adoption of ML-SDM. Climate change, land-use change, biological invasions, and biodiversity conservation affect ecosystems worldwide, but environmental conditions differ greatly among regions. For instance, The United States spans multiple climate zones, and frequent natural disturbances such as droughts, wildfires, and extreme storms have led to long-term dynamic changes in species distributions.\citep{turner2010disturbance}. China has witnessed continuous changes in land use, with such changes accelerating significantly after the 1990s. Along with the rapid urbanization and industrialization process, natural habitats have been affected to varying degrees \citep{liu1999framework,liu2010spatial}. These differences require ML models to be evaluated and adapted under diverse ecological settings. International collaboration may facilitate methodological exchange, data sharing, and dissemination of modelling practices across regions. However, the bibliometric patterns observed here do not directly demonstrate that collaboration improves model robustness, calibration, or spatial transferability. Actual improvements in transferability require independent spatial validation, evaluation under novel environmental conditions, and explicit assessment of uncertainty. In the same time, African and South American countries have relatively limited research output, but their unique arid and semi-arid ecosystems support many endemic species adapted to extreme environments  \citep{myers2000biodiversity}. For instance, arid ecosystems in the Sahara–Sahel region and the Namib Desert have evolved under long-term climatic change and severe water scarcity. This has resulted in highly specialized species assemblages and unique ecological processes \citep{le1997climate,henschel2008ecophysiology}. The highly endemic biodiversity pattern formed in the Atacama Desert, Caatinga Arid Forest and the Andes and Patagonia regions of South America under the combined effect of extreme arid conditions, complex terrain and significant climate gradients \citep{azua2012life,leal2005changing}. These areas not only have significant ecological research value, but also play a crucial role at the global scale in maintaining the integrity of ecosystems and protecting biodiversity \citep{myers2000biodiversity,ceballos2015accelerated}. Their biodiversity hotspots provide valuable opportunities for testing model generalization and expanding the global applicability of ML-SDM. Continued data sharing and international collaboration will therefore remain essential for future methodological development.

Research institutions serve as important centres of methodological innovation in ML-SDM, extending beyond their role as contributors to publication output \citep{wuchty2007increasing}. They contribute to algorithm development, model evaluation, and interdisciplinary collaboration. The differences between publication output and accumulated citation counts indicate that these two indicators capture different aspects of institutional bibliometric activity. Some institutions are characterized by high publication output, whereas others accumulate relatively high citation counts from a smaller number of publications. Collaboration among institutions may provide opportunities for methodological exchange, data sharing, and comparative evaluation across regions. The increasing availability of open datasets, shared computational platforms, and reproducible workflows enables ML-SDM models to be evaluated across diverse ecological regions. Such collaborative practices may provide opportunities to improve model robustness, support the assessment of spatial transferability, and facilitate the broader application of ML-SDM approaches in biodiversity assessment and conservation studies.

Scientific journals are important channels for methodological dissemination rather than simple publication outlets. The concentration of ML-SDM studies in ecology, biogeography, and ecological modelling journals indicates that the field has developed a stable knowledge dissemination system. Journals such as Methods in Ecology and Evolution and Ecological Modelling mainly publish methodological advances, while Ecological Informatics and Ecology and Evolution emphasize ecological applications in biodiversity conservation, environmental prediction, and ecosystem management. This publication pattern reflects the gradual transition from algorithm development to ecological decision support. Scientific journals also facilitate methodological dissemination through model evaluation, data sharing, and reproducible research. These mechanisms may accelerate the diffusion and validation of new ML-SDM approaches across the international research community.

Author-level analysis revealed different patterns of publication activity and citation accumulation in ML-SDM research. Some authors were associated with a relatively small number of publications but high accumulated TGCS and TLCS values, whereas others showed higher and more sustained publication output. It should be noted that citation accumulation is strongly influenced by publication age, as earlier studies generally have longer periods to accumulate citations. Therefore, differences in TGCS and TLCS among authors are interpreted as differences in citation accumulation patterns and bibliometric visibility rather than direct indicators of research quality or methodological importance. For example, Phillips SJ accumulated 22,269 TGCS and 6,004 TLCS, with a substantial proportion of these citations associated with a small number of publications in the dataset. His 2006 study published in Ecological Modelling, “Maximum entropy modeling of species geographic distributions” \citep{phillips2006maximum}, received 14,211 TGCS and 3,443 TLCS. This study made an important methodological contribution by introducing and applying the MaxEnt framework to presence-only species distribution modelling, and it subsequently accumulated substantial citations within and beyond the ML-SDM literature. Similarly, Elith J accumulated high citation counts, and the 2011 study “A statistical explanation of MaxEnt for ecologists” \citep{elith2011statistical} contributed to the methodological interpretation of ML-SDM by providing a statistical explanation of MaxEnt and clarifying its modelling framework. This publication also accumulated substantial citations across the scientific literature. In contrast, Peterson AT contributed 38 publications and accumulated 8,715 TGCS, representing a different pattern characterized by sustained publication activity over a longer period. Therefore, concentrated citation accumulation among some publications and sustained long-term publication activity represent two distinct author-level patterns in this field.

The country, institution, author, and journal networks together reveal a relatively stable bibliometric structure of ML-SDM research. The observed concentration of highly connected authors and institutions suggests that methodological contributions are associated with a relatively limited number of active research groups. These institutions showed substantial contributions to ML-SDM publications and collaborative activities. Scientific journals provided important publication venues for ML-SDM-related studies, while international collaboration patterns indicate contributions from researchers across diverse ecosystems and geographical regions. ML-SDM approaches have been widely applied to biodiversity conservation, climate-change assessment, biological invasions, species recovery, and conservation planning \citep{guisan2005predicting, elith2009species, franklin2013species}. Therefore, the multi-level research network provides insights into the organization and evolution of ML-SDM-related knowledge production rather than representing a direct causal pathway \citep{chen2017science, zupic2015bibliometric, donthu2021conduct}. It reflects interconnected patterns among methodological development, research collaboration, publication activities, and ecological applications represented in the literature. The observed evolution of ML-SDM coincides with advances in ML algorithms, increasing availability of environmental data resources, international collaboration, and expanded knowledge dissemination channels, which may have provided enabling conditions for continued methodological development \citep{telenius2011biodiversity, fick2017worldclim, reichstein2019deep}.

\subsection{Methodological evolution and knowledge accumulation}

The co-citation analysis and bibliographic coupling analysis provide complementary perspectives on the knowledge structure and recent methodological development of ML-SDM. Figure \ref{fig_mom9} identifies the intellectual foundations formed by frequently co-cited studies, highlighting the establishment of major modelling frameworks and evaluation concepts. In contrast, Figure \ref{fig_mom10} reveals recent research connections among publications sharing common references, thereby indicating the major methodological concerns addressed by contemporary ML-SDM studies. Together with Tables \ref{tab:method_milestones} and \ref{tab:model_comparison}, these analyses suggest that ML-SDM development involves continuous refinement of model formulation, evaluation strategies, and data representation rather than replacement of one algorithm by another.

First, the theoretical foundation of ML-SDM lies in quantifying species–environment relationships rather than simply improving prediction accuracy. Modern SDMs are associated with both Grinnellian and Hutchinsonian niche concepts: the former emphasizes the influence of environmental conditions on species geographic distributions, whereas the latter describes species niches within multidimensional environmental spaces\citep{grinnell1917niche,hutchinson1957cold}. In practice, most ML-SDM approaches are generally formulated within the Hutchinsonian niche framework by modelling species–environment relationships using multidimensional environmental predictors. Because most ML-SDM approaches are calibrated using observed occurrence records, their predictions mainly represent statistical associations related to the realized niche rather than direct estimates of the fundamental niche. Therefore, ML-SDM remains primarily a correlative modelling framework that aims to identify stable relationships between species occurrences and environmental conditions.

Early methodological contributions summarized in Table \ref{tab:method_milestones} mainly addressed the challenges associated with presence-only data and model evaluation. \cite{phillips2006maximum} established MaxEnt as a cornerstone of ML-SDM by introducing maximum entropy modelling with regularization to control model complexity. Later studies demonstrated the importance of transferability, sample size, background selection, and model evaluation. \cite{pearson2007predicting} highlighted spatial transferability as an essential criterion for SDM evaluation, while \cite{elith2011statistical} provided a statistical interpretation of MaxEnt by clarifying the roles of regularization, features, and background selection. These studies shifted ML-SDM from simple prediction toward more rigorous model construction and ecological interpretation.

Second, the representative approaches summarized in Table \ref{tab:model_comparison} demonstrate how different algorithmic frameworks provide alternative solutions for representing species–environment relationships. MaxEnt represents a widely adopted presence-background modelling framework because it provides a flexible framework for modelling presence-background data and controlling model complexity through regularization. However, its predictions are sensitive to feature-class and regularization settings, and inappropriate parameterization may lead to overly complex or simple models. In addition, MaxEnt outputs primarily represent relative environmental suitability or relative occurrence, while their interpretation as absolute occurrence probabilities requires additional assumptions (Table \ref{tab:model_comparison}).

Random Forest provides a different modelling strategy by capturing nonlinear relationships and predictor interactions through ensembles of decision trees \citep{cutler2007random}. Bootstrap sampling enables out-of-bag observations to be used for internal prediction-error estimation. However, conventional variable-importance measures can be biased toward predictors with particular characteristics, and correlations among environmental variables can distort permutation-based importance estimates (Table \ref{tab:model_comparison}). \cite{mi2017choose} further compared Random Forest with other SDM approaches under small-sample and undersampled conditions and evaluated its predictions using independent tracking data. Boosted Regression Trees provide another tree-based strategy by sequentially adding trees that focus on prediction errors from previous iterations. This allows flexible representation of nonlinear responses and predictor interactions, but model performance depends on coordinated tuning of learning rate, tree complexity, and the number of trees \citep{elith2008working}. Ensemble modelling represents a complementary development rather than an independent replacement of individual algorithms. By combining predictions from multiple models or from models fitted to different predictor subsets, ensemble approaches can reduce dependence on a single model specification and provide a way to examine variation among alternative predictions \citep{araujo2007ensemble,breiner2015overcoming}. All aforementioned developments indicate that the evolution of ML-SDM involves the adaptation of modelling strategies to different ecological data conditions rather than a simple replacement of algorithms.

Third, model evaluation and uncertainty analysis have become central components of the ML-SDM knowledge framework (Table \ref{tab:method_milestones}, Figures \ref{fig_mom9} and \ref{fig_mom10}). Early studies mainly focused on improving predictive performance, whereas later research recognized that high predictive accuracy does not necessarily indicate reliable ecological inference. Sampling bias, spatial autocorrelation, variable collinearity, and model complexity can strongly influence model outcomes. These limitations indicate that improving predictive accuracy alone is insufficient. For example, spatial filtering and environmentally independent validation can reduce overly optimistic performance estimates caused by sampling bias and spatial autocorrelation. Similarly, extrapolation assessments are necessary when models are applied beyond the environmental conditions represented in calibration data. Related studies further showed that background selection, pseudo-absence design, predictor collinearity, and environmental novelty can affect model calibration and transferred predictions \citep{phillips2009sample,barbet2012selecting,de2018evaluating,feng2019collinearity}. \cite{boria2014spatial} demonstrated that spatial filtering can reduce sampling bias, while ENMeval-based studies improved parameter optimization and spatially independent evaluation of MaxEnt models \citep{muscarella2014enm,kass2021enmeval}. These advances indicate an expansion from predictive performance alone toward greater attention to model reliability, reproducibility, spatial validation, and transferability.

Recent DL approaches introduce additional mechanisms for representing complex and spatially structured environmental information. However, their increased complexity does not necessarily indicate improved ecological understanding, because model interpretation, uncertainty quantification, and data requirements remain important limitations. As summarized in Table \ref{tab:model_comparison}, CNN, Vision Transformer, and Graph Neural Network (GNN)-based approaches differ mainly in how they encode spatial and relational information. CNN-based, Vision Transformer-based, and GNN-based approaches introduce different mechanisms for representing spatial and complex environmental information. CNN-based models can learn spatial patterns from environmental tensors and remote-sensing imagery, Vision Transformers use self-attention to model relationships among image patches, and GNN-based approaches represent relational information among landscape units through graph structures \citep{deneu2021convolutional,estopinan2022deep,hu2025introduction,wu2025gnn}. However, DL models remain mainly correlative when trained with occurrence and environmental data. Their improved predictive performance does not automatically indicate better representation of ecological mechanisms. In addition, increased model complexity introduces challenges related to interpretability, uncertainty assessment, and data requirements.

Overall, the methodological landscape of ML-SDM can be understood as an interconnected framework involving model formulation, algorithmic strategies, evaluation procedures, spatial validation, and uncertainty assessment. The co-citation network (Figure \ref{fig_mom9}) reveals the shared intellectual foundations underlying these components, whereas the bibliographic coupling network (Figure \ref{fig_mom10}) highlights recent research fronts and methodological concerns addressed by contemporary studies. Table \ref{tab:method_milestones} provides representative publications illustrating major methodological contributions, while Table \ref{tab:model_comparison} summarizes the distinct capabilities and limitations of representative ML-SDM approaches. Together, these analyses indicate that ML-SDM development reflects a progressive broadening of methodological attention from model construction and prediction toward reliability assessment, spatial generalization, uncertainty quantification, and ecological interpretation. Rather than representing a simple replacement of one algorithm by another, methodological advances in ML-SDM involve the interaction between ecological objectives, data characteristics, modelling approaches, and evaluation frameworks.

\multicolumn{4}{@{}l}{
\textbf{Traditional machine-learning species distribution models (TML-SDM)}
}\\
\addlinespace[3pt]

MaxEnt &
1.Estimates relative environmental suitability by contrasting environmental conditions at occurrence locations with those available across the background using a maximum-entropy formulation.
2.Feature classes provide flexible representations of species–environment relationships, while built-in regularization explicitly controls model complexity and reduces overfitting. &
1.Model complexity is sensitive to feature-class and regularization settings, default configurations may produce over-complex or over-simplified models.
2.MaxEnt outputs primarily quantify relative suitability/relative occurrence, interpreting the default logistic output as absolute occurrence probability requires additional assumptions about prevalence and sampling. &
\citep{merow2013practical,elith2011statistical,warren2011ecological,low2021predictor,guillera2014maxent,yackulic2013presence,morales2017maxent} \\

Random Forest &
1.Combines bootstrap-aggregated decision trees with random predictor subsampling, enabling nonlinear species–environment relationships and complex predictor interactions to be represented without specifying a parametric response function.
2.Bootstrap sampling provides out-of-bag observations for internal prediction-error estimation, while built-in variable-importance measures allow environmental predictors to be ranked. &
1.Standard RF variable-importance measures may favour predictors with more possible split points or more categories, potentially producing biased importance rankings.
2.Strong correlations among predictors can distort conventional permutation importance because marginal permutation breaks the dependence structure among variables. &
\citep{cutler2007random,pichler2023machine,boulesteix2012overview,biau2016random,strobl2007bias,strobl2008conditional} \\

Boosted Regression Trees &
Builds an additive ensemble of regression trees through forward stagewise boosting, with successive trees focusing on prediction errors left by previous trees; this enables flexible modelling of nonlinear responses and predictor interactions without prespecifying their functional form. &
1. Regularization requires coordinated tuning of learning rate, tree complexity, and number of trees, whose optimal combination depends on the data and interaction complexity.
2. Because trees are added sequentially, boosting can continue reducing training error beyond the point of optimal generalization; cross-validation or independent validation is therefore needed to select the number of trees and control overfitting &
\citep{elith2008working,pichler2023machine,natekin2013gradient}\\

\addlinespace

\multicolumn{4}{@{}l}{
\textbf{Deep-learning species distribution models (DL-SDM)}
}\\
\addlinespace[3pt]
CNN-based SDM &
1.Uses convolution over geospatial environmental tensors or image patches to preserve and learn the spatial configuration of environmental conditions surrounding occurrence locations, rather than relying only on point-wise covariates.
2.Can learn predictive features directly from high-resolution remote-sensing imagery and can be extended to multi-temporal image stacks to incorporate seasonal environmental dynamics. &
1.Requires predictors to be represented as spatial tensors or image patches, making spatial extent, raster resolution, and cross-layer alignment additional modelling choices that determine the environmental context available to the CNN.
2.Patch-based inputs increase susceptibility to spatial information leakage when training and test neighbourhoods overlap or are spatially close, potentially inflating evaluation performance; spatial validation therefore needs to account for the footprint of CNN input patches. &
\citep{deneu2021convolutional,estopinan2022deep,kellenberger2026performance,deneu2022very,gillespie2024deep} \\

Vision Transformer-based SDM &
Divides environmental imagery into patches and uses positional encoding and self-attention to learn dependencies among image-patch representations, providing a non-convolutional mechanism for extracting spatial features from environmental imagery. &
1.Vision transformer models generally require large training datasets and carefully designed training strategies to achieve competitive performance, and transfer or self-supervised pretraining is often preferable to training from scratch.
2.In the evaluated plant-community case, Vision transformer models achieved lower predictive performance than both CNN and multilayer perceptron models, so a general advantage over alternative SDM architectures has not yet been established&
\citep{hu2025introduction,chen2024mitree,zhang2022multimodal} \\

GNN-based SDM &
1.Represents ecological entities such as locations, landscape patches, or species as graph nodes and their spatial or detection relationships as edges, allowing message passing to model relational dependencies that are not explicitly represented by point-wise environmental covariates.
2.Graph representations can encode irregular or heterogeneous ecological relationships rather than requiring environmental context to be represented on a regular raster neighbourhood. &
GNN-SDM requires an explicit graph representation, so node definitions, edge construction, and neighbourhood structure become additional modelling decisions that can influence the information propagated through the network. &
\citep{wu2025gnn,corso2024graph} \\

\subsection{Evolution toward DL-SDM: opportunities and limitations}

Keyword evolution and Tables \ref{tab:method_milestones}, \ref{tab:model_comparison} indicate that the development of ML-SDM is not a replacement of traditional ML by DL. Instead, it represents an expansion of modelling capability coinciding with increasing data complexity and advances in computational technologies. Since 2006, ML-SDM research has increasingly incorporated approaches for handling high-dimensional ecological data and multi-source environmental information \citep{pichler2023machine}. This transition reflects an extension of modelling capacity rather than a complete change of modelling paradigm.

(1) From nonlinear prediction to high-dimensional ecological modelling.

Early ML-SDM mainly focused on establishing nonlinear relationships between species occurrences and structured environmental variables. MaxEnt and Random Forest became two representative approaches during this period (Figure \ref{fig_mom11}). MaxEnt provided an effective solution for presence-only modelling and performed well under limited occurrence data \citep{phillips2006maximum}. Random Forest provided an alternative framework for representing nonlinear relationships of nonlinear relationships and variable interactions through ensemble decision trees \citep{mi2017choose}. However, these approaches usually depend on manually selected predictors and predefined feature representations. Model performance is strongly influenced by the quality and availability of environmental variables. Most inputs are structured datasets containing climate, topography, and land-use variables.

Recent keyword evolution shows increasing attention toward DL, remote sensing, and ensemble modelling (Figures \ref{fig_mom12} and \ref{fig_mom13}). These trends indicate a shift toward multi-source and high-dimensional ecological data integration. DL-SDM extends traditional approaches through automatic feature learning. CNN-based models can extract spatial patterns from environmental grids, while GNN-based approaches provide new opportunities to represent spatial connections among landscape units \citep{deneu2021convolutional, wu2025gnn}. Therefore, the transition from TML-SDM to DL-SDM represents an expansion of modelling capability rather than a replacement of previous approaches. Traditional ML remains valuable for small datasets, structured predictors, and studies requiring clear ecological interpretation. DL provides additional capacity for complex data structures and large-scale environmental information.

(2) DL improves prediction but does not automatically improve ecological understanding.

Although DL models can capture complex nonlinear relationships and spatial patterns, they remain primarily correlative approaches when trained with species occurrence and environmental data. Similarly, predictive performance should be distinguished from model calibration. A model may achieve high discrimination metrics while still producing poorly calibrated predictions or unreliable suitability estimates under different environmental conditions \citep{ryo2021explainable}. Improved prediction performance does not automatically indicate better representation of ecological mechanisms. DL models can identify complex statistical associations between species distributions and environmental conditions. However, they are difficult reveal physiological mechanisms, species interactions, or ecological processes underlying distribution patterns. This limitation is particularly relevant to SDM applications because model predictions are often interpreted in ecological terms and used to support biodiversity assessment and conservation decision-making. Traditional ML models may provide comparatively accessible diagnostic information through approaches such as variable importance analysis, although these measures also require careful interpretation. In contrast, DNN generate hierarchical feature representations, which increase model complexity and reduce transparency. CNN, Transformer, and GNN architectures improve spatial pattern recognition but also introduce stronger black-box characteristics \citep{zhang2020three, hu2025introduction, wu2025gnn}. Therefore, future DL-SDM development should focus not only on predictive performance but also on ecological interpretability and scientific reliability.

(3) Interpretability and uncertainty as future priorities.

As ML-SDM becomes increasingly applied in biodiversity assessment and conservation planning, trustworthy prediction has become more important. Future research should improve model reliability.

First, explainable artificial intelligence methods should be integrated into DL-SDM. Approaches such as shapley additive explanations (SHAP), attention mechanisms, and feature attribution methods can help identify influential predictors and improve model interpretation \citep{huang2025identification, du2025revealing}. Second, uncertainty assessment should become a standard component of ML-SDM. Model uncertainty originates from multiple sources, including data uncertainty, model uncertainty, and extrapolation uncertainty. Data uncertainty is associated with occurrence data quality, sampling bias, and spatial coverage. Model uncertainty results from algorithm selection, parameter settings, and model structure. Extrapolation uncertainty occurs when models are transferred to novel environmental conditions \citep{meyer2016multidimensional, vrugt2003shuffled, yanlong2020challenges}. Therefore, future evaluation frameworks should move beyond single performance metrics and consider predictive accuracy, interpretability, and uncertainty simultaneously.

(4) Spatial transferability and multi-source ecological data.

Spatial transferability remains a major challenge for ML-SDM \citep{yanlong2020challenges}. Many studies calibrate and evaluate models within limited geographic regions. However, models with high predictive accuracy in one region may fail under novel environmental conditions. Applications such as global biodiversity assessment require evaluation of whether models maintain reliability across regions and spatial scales. Future research should focus on three directions. First, cross-region validation should be strengthened to evaluate model generalization. Second, domain adaptation approaches may provide potential approaches for addressing transferability challenges among different ecological regions. Third, the integration of multi-source ecological data and foundation models may provide new opportunities for combining remote sensing, climate information, land-use dynamics, and species relationships within unified prediction frameworks.

Overall, DL-SDM represents an important transition from manual feature engineering toward data-driven feature learning in species distribution modelling. However, the future of ML-SDM should not be defined by increasingly complex models alone. The primary goal should be the development of trustworthy ecological prediction frameworks that integrate predictive performance, calibration, spatial transferability, ecological interpretation, and uncertainty assessment.


\section{Conclusion}

This study provides a systematic overview of the technological evolution of ML-SDM from 2006 to 2025 by integrating bibliometric analysis with methodological synthesis. The results demonstrate that ML-SDM has undergone a progressive expansion of modelling capability rather than a simple replacement of traditional approaches by DL. Its development has coincided with methodological innovation, increasing ecological demands, expanding environmental data resources, and advances in computational technologies. The main conclusions are summarized as follows.

(1) Temporal analysis revealed that ML-SDM has undergone a continuous evolution in both methodological approaches and research themes. Early studies primarily focused on establishing ML-based prediction frameworks, with MaxEnt and random forest becoming foundational approaches for species distribution modelling. Subsequent research increasingly emphasized model evaluation, sampling bias correction, uncertainty assessment, and ecological applicability. Since 2020, emerging themes including DL, remote sensing, ensemble modelling, and multi-source environmental data have become increasingly prominent, indicating a transition from improving predictive performance toward integrating more complex ecological information.

(2) ML-SDM research expanded rapidly during 2006–2025 and developed into a globally connected research landscape. The United States and China were the leading contributors in publication output, while major institutions, productive authors, and core journals formed prominent nodes in the research network. The emergence, development, and global dissemination of ML-SDM were associated with growing ecological demands, advances in computational technologies, expanding biodiversity and environmental data resources, and increasing international collaboration. These patterns indicate that ML-SDM development occurred alongside broader ecological, technological, and collaborative changes.

(3) Methodological evolution and knowledge accumulation analysis demonstrated that methodological innovation in ML-SDM has accumulated through successive solutions to ecological modelling challenges rather than through the emergence of individual algorithms. Co-citation analysis highlighted the intellectual foundations established by early MaxEnt-based studies and ecological modelling frameworks, whereas bibliographic coupling revealed recent methodological diversification involving ensemble approaches, DL, and spatially explicit modelling. This evolutionary pathway reflects a gradual shift from algorithm selection toward integrated modelling frameworks capable of addressing complex ecological data characteristics.

(4) Despite substantial methodological advances, ML-SDM still faces challenges in improving ecological reliability. Current approaches remain primarily correlative models based on species–environment associations and may not directly represent underlying ecological mechanisms. Increasing model complexity does not necessarily translate into improved ecological understanding. Future developments should therefore focus on improving ecological interpretation, uncertainty quantification, spatial transferability, and model transparency to enhance the reliability of ML-SDM applications.

(5) DL-SDM represents an important extension of ML-SDM by enabling the analysis of high-dimensional, spatially structured, and heterogeneous ecological data. Through multi-layer architectures and automatic feature learning, DL-SDM expands modelling capability beyond conventional feature-based ML approaches and provides new opportunities for integrating remote sensing, spatial information, and complex environmental variables. However, traditional ML approaches remain valuable when sample size, interpretability, and computational efficiency are critical considerations. Therefore, future ML-SDM development will likely depend on the complementary integration of traditional and DL approaches.

(6) Although DL-SDM provides enhanced predictive capacity, its practical application remains constrained by several challenges, including high data requirements, limited ecological interpretability, uncertainty propagation, and reduced transferability across spatial and environmental contexts. Future research should therefore move toward trustworthy DL-SDM frameworks that combine advanced representation learning with ecological knowledge, uncertainty assessment, and transparent modelling strategies.
